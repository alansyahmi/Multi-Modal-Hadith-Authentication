CHAPTER 4: EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN

4.1 Introduction

This chapter lays out the experiments we designed to test each of the three research objectives — developing a quantitative reliability index from scholarly judgments (PO1), building a Knowledge Graph with GNN-based structural analysis (PO2), and evaluating the framework against established Hadith gradings (PO3). Every experiment is organised to mirror the three-stage pipeline from Chapter 3, so the link between method and objective stays clear throughout.

I cover the full experimental environment — hardware, software, datasets — before walking through each experiment in turn: what we set out to learn, how we designed the test, what parameters matter, and what we expected to find. Where the actual implementation ended up diverging from the idealised methodology, I flag it and explain why. The aim is to give anyone reading this a complete, repeatable account of every experiment.

4.3 Data Preparation Experiments

Before any entity extraction or graph building could happen, the raw data had to be cleaned and cross-referenced. These first two experiments tackle exactly that — parsing biographical volumes from classical sources, then aligning Hadith records across two independently collected datasets. Both feed directly into PO1 and PO2.

4.3.1 Experiment 1: Biographical Text Parsing

What we wanted to know: Could a Regex-based parser reliably isolate individual biographical entries (tarājim) from the raw OpenITI volumes of Tahdhīb al-Kamāl and Taqrīb al-Tahdhīb?

How we tested it

The parser (src.shamela.narrator_parser.segment_volume) runs a multi-stage Regex pipeline over each raw volume. First it strips architectural metadata — YAML headers, page-number selectors, editorial comments. Then it hunts for entry boundaries using historical identifiers and narrator-name patterns. Every entry it finds gets a confidence label: high if the entry has a name plus teachers and grades, medium if the name comes with partial metadata, low if we only caught the name. We ran this against all 35 volumes of Tahdhīb al-Kamāl and the single Taqrīb al-Tahdhīb volume, then merged everything into one unified narrator encyclopedia.

Key parameters

What we expected

A structured JSON export of every extracted entry — entry ID, volume, name, full text, teachers, students, grades, death info, and confidence. Our target was high-confidence extraction for at least 70% of entries. The rest would inevitably land in medium or low due to orthographic ambiguity and all the structural quirks you get in centuries-old manuscript transcriptions.

4.3.2 Experiment 2: Two-Tier Alignment Framework

What we wanted to know: could we reliably cross-reference Hadith records between the Sanadset 650K corpus and the HuggingFace Kutub as-Sitta dataset, despite them having no shared index?

How we tested it

The pipeline (src.alignment.hybrid_aligner) works exactly as described in Section 3.2.3. Tier 1 uses a sparse TF-IDF vectorizer with character n-grams to cut the search space from 650K candidates down to a top-10 shortlist per query. Tier 2 then runs rapidfuzz partial_ratio with a 95% threshold to confirm the match. Anything that falls short of that bar gets quarantined for manual review. We tested this on a stratified sample of 1,200 Hadiths — 200 from each of the six canonical books — with a fixed seed of 42 so the exact same subset appears every time.

Key parameters

What we expected

A JSON file of alignment results for all 1,200 queries — similarity score, TF-IDF score, match status, and quarantine flag. We aimed for at least 75% success rate on matches, with under 5% of records sent to quarantine.

4.4 Entity Extraction and Disambiguation Experiments

These experiments bridge Stages 1 and 2. Once the data is clean and aligned, the next question is how to identify narrators and link them to a canonical identity. That is the core of PO2.

4.4.1 Experiment 3: BERT Embedding Extraction for Biographical Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl Features

What we wanted to know: could deep contextual embeddings of the biographical text passages that contain Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl assessments give us useful semantic features for the GNN node representations?

How we tested it

The core methodological premise here is straightforward: the classical kutub ar-rijal contain qualitative judgements about each narrator's moral probity (ʿadāla) and mnemonic precision (ḍabṭ). These judgements are expressed through a highly technical vocabulary — Thiqa, Ṣadūq, Kaḏḏāb, Layyin — but also through longer contextual phrases that a simple keyword lookup might miss. The idea is to encode the full biographical text segment that contains the evaluative statement, rather than just the term itself.

The biographical entries parsed in Experiment 1 serve as the input corpus. For each narrator entry, the text segments that carry Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl content (typically preceded by phrases like "kāna..." or following the narrator's name) are extracted. These segments are fed through a CAMeL-BERT model (bert-base-arabic-camelbert-msa-sentiment) to produce [CLS] token embeddings that capture the semantic nuance of the scholarly assessment. The 768-dimensional output is PCA-reduced to 128 dimensions and attached as semantic node features in the Knowledge Graph, complementing the structural features derived from transmission network topology.

Key parameters

What we expected

An augmented graph file (pyg_graph_bert.npz) where each narrator node carries 8 graph-structural features plus 128-dimensional BERT embeddings of their biographical assessments — totalling 136 dimensions. Alongside this, a lexicon-only variant using the GRADE_SCORE_MAP (Experiment 7) for comparison in the GNN ablation study (Experiment 6).

4.4.2 Experiment 4: Narrator Name Matching and Encyclopedia Coverage

What we wanted to know: how well can we match Sanadset narrator names to the biographical encyclopedia?

How we tested it

We took narrators from the aligned Hadith chains, normalised them (strip diacritics, normalise alif variants, collapse yā and hā), and tried to find them in the narrator encyclopedia. Exact matches were accepted straight away. When a name did not match exactly, we fell back to fuzzy matching with rapidfuzz partial_ratio at a threshold of 80%. We tracked both the proportion of successful matches and, for the matched entries, whether the encyclopedia carried grades, death dates, and teacher-student lists.

Key parameters

What we expected

A JSON file (narrator_matches.json) with match status per narrator. Target: ≥ 80% overall match rate (exact + fuzzy combined) and at least 60% of those matched carrying grade annotations from Ibn Hajar's corpus.

4.5 Knowledge Graph Construction Experiments

4.5.1 Experiment 5: Graph Topology and Feature Engineering

What we wanted to know: how does the scale of the transmission network affect graph properties and downstream GNN performance?

How we tested it

We built the graph by walking through Sanadset rows, pulling narrator names from each chain, and creating directed edges to represent teacher-student relationships (teacher → student). Node features include log-transformed appearance count, in-degree, out-degree, average chain position, and the grade score from Ibn Hajar's 12-tier mapping. Edges carry positional data and frequency counts. We varied the number of Sanadset rows from 10,000 to 100,000 to see how node count, edge density, and degree distribution change with scale.

Grade scoring

Ibn Hajar's 12-tier system maps to continuous scores in [0.0, 1.0] via src/stage2/config.py. Companions (Ṣaḥāba) get 1.0; Thiqa Thiqa (supremely reliable) gets 0.95; Thiqa (trustworthy) gets 0.85; Ṣadūq (truthful) gets 0.70; and so on down to Kaḏḏāb (liar) and Waḍḍāʿ (fabricator) at 0.0. Narrators with unknown or missing grades default to 0.30.

Key parameters

What we expected

A PyTorch Geometric graph file (pyg_graph_large.npz) with ~50K–60K nodes and ~200K–300K edges. The graded variant (pyg_graph_large_graded.npz) would put ground-truth labels in the first feature column for semi-supervised GNN training.

4.6 GNN Architecture and Training Experiments

4.6.1 Experiment 6: Graph Convolutional Network Ablation Study

What we wanted to know: how much does adding semantic features from the biographical texts (BERT embeddings of Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl passages, GRADE_SCORE_MAP keyword scores) improve narrator reliability prediction over graph-structural features alone?

How we tested it

We built a two-layer GCN in PyTorch Geometric. The model reads node features and outputs a continuous reliability score in [0, 1] for each narrator. Three variants were compared: (a) graph-only — just the 8 structural features; (b) graph + BERT — structural features plus the 128-dimensional PCA-reduced embeddings; (c) graph + lexicon — structural features plus the GRADE_SCORE_MAP keyword score (1 feature from biographical Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl term extraction). Training is semi-supervised: 70% of nodes have their grade masked, and the model predicts them by propagating information from the 30% labelled nodes through the graph.

Hyperparameters

What we expected

A clean comparison of MAE, R², Spearman and Pearson correlations, and binary accuracy (threshold at ≥ 0.65 for "accepted"). The ablation would show us whether the extra semantic modalities add anything over the pure graph-structural signal.

4.7 Trust Scoring and Evaluation Experiments

These experiments close the loop on PO1 and PO3 — taking scores from the GNN and turning them into something that maps onto the traditional Ṣaḥīḥ/Ḥasan/Ḍaʿīf/Mawḍūʿ categories.

4.7.1 Experiment 7: Semantic Sentiment Quantisation of Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl Terms

What we wanted to know: can the qualitative Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl terms extracted from the biographical encyclopedia be reliably mapped to continuous reliability scores that reflect the classical scholarly hierarchy?

How we tested it

This experiment operationalises the core idea behind PO1: translating the classical critics' vocabulary into a numerical index. The GRADE_SCORE_MAP (defined in src/stage2/config.py) assigns a continuous score in [0.0, 1.0] to each term in Ibn Hajar al-ʿAsqalānī's 12-tier grading system. The mapping follows the classical hierarchy: terms of supreme praise (Thiqa Thiqa, Thiqa Ḥāfiẓ) sit at the top; terms of trustworthiness (Thiqa, Ma'mūn) occupy the 0.85 band; Ṣadūq and its variants fall around 0.65–0.70; criticism terms like Ḍaʿīf sit at 0.15; and severe condemnations (Kaḏḏāb, Waḍḍāʿ, Dajāl) land at 0.0. Narrators with no explicit grade default to 0.30.

Two tracks are planned per the methodology in Section 3.4.1. Track B — the static keyword lookup using this mapping — is the one currently implemented. When the biographical parser extracts a grade term from a narrator's entry (e.g., "Thiqa" from Taqrīb al-Tahdhīb), the system looks it up in the map and assigns the corresponding score to that narrator node in the Knowledge Graph. If multiple assessments exist for the same narrator, the scores can be aggregated via weighted mean or Bayesian posterior estimation. Track A — a transformer-based (AraBERT v2) regression model that reads the full contextual biographical sentence rather than just the keyword — is planned for FYP2. The current experiment validates the Track B output distribution and establishes the baseline for comparison.

4.7.2 Experiment 8: Chain-Level Reliability Scoring

What we wanted to know: can the full pipeline — from sanad text to final Hadith grade — produce scores that agree with the canonical grades in Kutub as-Sitta?

How we tested it

The scoring pipeline (hadith_authenticator.py) works in four steps. First, it parses the sanad string into a list of narrator names. Second, it looks each name up in the Knowledge Graph to get the GNN-predicted reliability score. Third, it computes chain-level statistics — mean, minimum, variance. Fourth, it maps the aggregate to a grade using thresholds we calibrated against the 1,200-sample dataset from Experiment 2. We then compare predicted grades against the ground-truth Grade column in the HuggingFace data.

Grade thresholds

What we expected

For all 1,200 evaluation Hadiths, a predicted grade plus confidence score. The overall evaluation uses accuracy, macro-averaged F1, RMSE (against normalised ordinal grades), and Cohen's Kappa. A naive majority-class baseline would tell us whether the full pipeline is actually adding value.

4.7.3 Experiment 9: Multi-Sanad Matn Analysis

What we wanted to know: when the same matn appears with different narrator chains, how well do their scores agree — and does the aggregate give us something more reliable than any single chain?

How we tested it

We normalised matn texts from the Sanadset corpus and grouped them by SHA-256 hash. Groups that had three or more distinct narrator chains were pulled out for deeper analysis. Each chain was scored independently through the GNN pipeline. Then we computed aggregate scores four ways: simple mean of chain means, best-path (the strongest single chain), consensus score (which penalises high variance across chains), and worst-chain (weakest-link pessimism). Results got categorised into unanimous strong, unanimous weak, and conflicted groups.
