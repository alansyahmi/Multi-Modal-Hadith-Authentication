ABSTRACT

The authentication of Prophetic reports (Ḥadīth) through the science of narrator criticism (ʿIlm ar‑Rijal/Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl) is a cornerstone of Islamic scholarship, yet the digital era has outpaced manual verification methods. This project proposes a multi‑modal framework that automates narrator reliability scoring by integrating three computational modalities: Arabic Named Entity Recognition (NER) using a fine‑tuned AraBERT v2 model to extract narrator names, kunya, and nisba from classical biographical texts (Taqrīb al-Tahḏīb of Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalāni and the Tahḏīb al-Kamāl of al-Mizzī) and isnād chains (AR‑Sanad 280K); a Knowledge Graph (KG) constructed with NetworkX (~1,500 narrator nodes, verbal edges e.g. heard_from and reported_from, rich attributes including tabaqa, travel history, and classical Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl labels) to enable structural and temporal integrity checks; and semantic sentiment quantisation that maps qualitative terms (e.g., Thiqa, Ṣadūq, Kaḏḏāb) to continuous reliability weights (0.00–1.00) and applies edge‑level risk modifiers (e.g., 0.80 for tadlīs). The final chain score is computed as the product of node weights and edge modifiers, then mapped to Ṣaḥīḥ/Ḥasan/Ḍaʿīf/Mawḍūʿ grades. Evaluation against ground‑truth gradings from the Kutub as‑Sitta (the Six Canonical Hadith Collections; i.e. Ṣaḥīḥ al‑Bukhārī, Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Sunan Abī Dāwūd, Jāmiʿ at‑Tirmiḏī, Sunan al‑Nasāʾī and Sunan Ibn Mājah) uses accuracy, F1, RMSE, and Cohen’s Kappa. The framework addresses the research gap of end‑to‑end automated trust scoring, providing an explainable, reproducible decision‑support tool for Ḥadith scholars.

Keywords: Hadith authentication, ʿIlm ar‑Rijal, Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl, Arabic NER, Knowledge Graph, Graph Neural Networks, semantic sentiment quantisation, multi‑modal framework

CHAPTER 1

INTRODUCTION

1.1 Overview

The authentication of prophetic traditions (Sunna) through the reports attributed to the Prophet Muhammad (Hadith) forms one of the most intricate scholarly endeavours in classical Islamic civilisation. To achieve such rigorous requirements, the science of evaluating the men and women who form the chains of narration (isnād) through which a Hadith is transmitted is then employed. In Arabic, this science is known as ʿIlm ar-Rijal. Additionally, an inner layer of the science known as the discipline of Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl (literally "criticism and commendation") provides the epistemological framework by which scholars assess the moral probity (ʿadālah) and mnemonic precision (ḍabṭ) of every individual narrator in a given chain (Kamali, 2014). Over the centuries, this civilisational practice produced vast historical corpora of biographical literature, such as Ibn Hajar al-ʿAsqalāni's twelve-volume Tahdhīb al-Tahdhīb, representing thousands of qualitative evaluative judgements that collectively determine the trustworthiness of the prophetic record.

The digitisation of classical texts through global repositories in recent years has made the Hadith corpus accessible on an unprecedented scale. Yet paradoxically, this very accessibility has created a new challenge; the capacity of modern scholarship to rigorously authenticate and verify Hadith at the speed of digital dissemination has not kept pace with the volume of their circulation. Recent work by Mosa (2025) highlights a pervasive issue of general anxiety, wherein the rapidity of digital access frequently surpasses the capacity to authenticate transmission chains. The manual cross-referencing labour of biographical entries across hundreds of classical volumes remains an expert-dependent process that is resistant to scale, thus presenting a compounding challenge for the field as a whole (Mosa, 2025).

In hopes of closing such gaps, this project proposes to develop a multi-modal and data-driven framework that automates and hopefully streamlines the whole process of narrator reliability evaluation while being grounded in the classical principles of Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl. The framework shall also aim to integrate these three advanced computational techniques:

Transformer-based Arabic Named Entity Recognition (NER) for the precise extraction and disambiguation of narrator identities from classical texts (Muther et al., 2023);

Graph Neural Networks (GNN) for modelling the structural and temporal integrity of isnād transmission chains (Mahmoud et al., 2024); and

Semantic Sentiment Quantisation to translate qualitative scholarly evaluations into a standardised, continuous reliability index (Ghriss, 2025). 

The integration of these methodologies is significantly informed by foundational studies, including Mahmoud et al. (2022) on narrator disambiguation, Farooqi et al. (2024) on structured transmission datasets, and sequence-based isnād modelling by Najeeb (2022) and Oumer (2025). This research seeks to develop a reproducible, explicable, and epistemologically robust system that directly enhances the traditional discipline of ʿIlm ar-Rijal through contemporary data science techniques.

1.2 Problem Background

The evaluation of Hadith authenticity through ʿIlm ar-Rijal remains a cornerstone of Islamic scholarship, as the credibility of a prophetic tradition is fundamentally tied to the reliability of its chain of transmission (isnād). This rigorous verification system necessitates that every link in the chain be scrutinised for both moral integrity (ʿadāla) and mnemonic precision (ḍabṭ) (Kamali, 2022). Traditionally, this process is a labour-intensive endeavour that requires scholars to manually cross-reference thousands of qualitative biographical entries across vast historical corpuses, such as the 12-volume Tahdhib at-Tahdhib of Ibn Hajar or the foundational Andalusian Maliki assessments in Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr’s At-Tamhid (Al-Asqalani, d. 852 AH; Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr, d. 463 AH).

Feasibility studies have confirmed that machine learning can indeed assist in automating isnād analysis, provided that the nuances of classical Arabic and the methodological standards of traditional jurisprudence are preserved (Usman, 2025; Mahmoud et al., 2022). The consonantal script and orthographic ambiguity of classical texts, however, make this particularly challenging, as minor variations in name orthography can lead to misidentification of narrators across manuscript traditions (El Ganadi et al., 2025). This results in an urgent need for Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques to extract and disambiguate narrator identities, and for Graph Neural Networks (GNN) to model the complex relational networks those narrators form (Farooqi et al., 2024; Mahmoud et al., 2022).

Recent work has confirmed the technical viability of this approach, where transformer-based models like AraBERT have achieved high accuracy in identifying ambiguous narrators from biographical text, while GNN frameworks can generate node embeddings to capture the relative position and credibility of a narrator within the broader transmission network (Ahmad et al., 2025; Mahmoud et al., 2022). By transitioning to a systematically quantified framework, researchers can achieve higher consistency and speed in evaluation without compromising the epistemological roots of the science (Tarmom, 2022).

1.3 Problem Statements

Two primary and significant challenges remain in the computational study of Hadith despite promising advancements in Arabic NLP:

While current models have improved in Named Entity Recognition (NER) for extracting narrator names and lineages, there is a critical lack of computational frameworks capable of quantifying narrator reliability. Identifying a narrator is insufficient, as the framework must be able to "judge" their standing based on historical evidence (Ahmad et al., 2025).

Recent work by Mosa (2025) and Oumer (2025) has shown that transformer models are capable of extracting narration chains from Hadith text as graphs. Nonetheless, those approaches stop at the graph, as they did not assign a reliability score to any narrator or use any actual Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl praise or criticism terms to judge narrator trustworthiness. This research goes beyond graph construction by adding a quantitative reliability index and GNN‑based structural analysis.

1.4 Project Objectives

The main objectives of this project are as follows,

To develop an NLP classification model that maps qualitative scholarly judgments to a standardised, quantitative reliability index (0.0 - 1.0).

To construct a Knowledge Graph representing teacher-student relationships and utilise GNNs supported with geospatial data to evaluate the structural integrity and geographical feasibility of transmission chains.

To evaluate the framework's accuracy by comparing automated reliability scores against established scholarly Hadith gradings (Ṣaḥīḥ, Ḥasan, Ḍaʿīf).

1.5 Project Scope

To ensure that the research remains focused, this project operates within a clearly defined set of boundaries, a technically feasible methodology, and is evaluable within the constraints of a single academic year.

1.5.1 Data Sources

The framework will be trained and tested exclusively on digitised classical Arabic biographical texts available through the OpenITI and Shamela repositories, specifically the Taqrīb al-Tahdhīb of Ibn Hajar al-Asqalāni and the Tahdhīb al-Kamāl of al-Mizzī. These texts contain the most comprehensive and widely referenced collections of narrator biographical assessments and thus represent the most suitable ground-truth source for Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl labels.

1.5.2 Scale and Dataset

The initial experimental scope targets approximately 1,500 narrator biographical entries for building the reliability model and 1,000 validated Hadith isnāds drawn from the Al-Kutub al-Sittah (The Six Canonical Books, i.e. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhari, Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Sunan Abī Dāwūd, Jāmiʿ at‑Tirmiḏī (Sunan at‑Tirmiḏī), Sunan al‑Nasāʾī and Sunan Ibn Mājah) for end-to-end testing. While this is a small scale compared to the hundreds of thousands of available data points, this scale remains consistent with comparable feasibility studies in the literature (Mahmoud et al., 2022; Najeeb, 2022) and is sufficiently large to support statistically meaningful evaluation without exhausting available computational resources.

1.5.3 Technical Scope

The system will focus on four interconnected tasks,

Narrator extraction: Identifying proper names, kunya (honorifics), and nisba (place-based identifiers) from isnād;

Entity disambiguation: Resolving alias variations to canonical narrator identities via a Knowledge Graph;

Reliability scoring: Mapping aggregated Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl labels to a continuous index . The primary programming language is Python, utilising Arabic-specific NLP libraries (e.g., CAMeL Tools, PyArabic) and transformer models (e.g., AraBERT, MARBERT);

Geospatial Mapping: Extracting narrator locations (nisba) and mapping them to coordinates to verify if a student and teacher were in the same region at the same time.

1.5.4 Application Domain Limitations

This research is strictly concerned with the structural evaluation of isnād (sanad criticism). The analysis of Hadith textual content (Matn criticism) requires comparative jurisprudential reasoning across the four Sunni schools and falls outside the scope of this project. Furthermore, any automated reliability score produced by the framework is intended as a decision-support tool for scholars, not as a definitive Islamic ruling or fatwa. The epistemological authority of qualified human muhaddithun is expressly preserved.

1.5.5 Out of Scope

The following are explicitly excluded from this project:

Matn (textual body) analysis or content-based Hadith grading,

Coverage of Shīʿa isnād evaluation methodologies,

Real-time or web-based deployment of the scoring pipeline, and

Languages other than Arabic (e.g. Urdu or Persian Hadith commentaries).

2.1 Hadith and its Structure

According to Almaany (n.d.), a Hadith (حَدِيث) is an Arabic noun that means a “report; account; tale; narrative; yarn” or a “talking; discourse; words; utterance; address; conversation; dialogue; saying; speaking; speech; talk”. Islamically, Hadith due to its nature as the embodiment of the direct speech, talk, words, actions, approvals, and attributes from the Prophet Muhammad himself, plays a very important role in reconstructing the Sunna (“practice”) as practiced by the Prophet and his companions (Shaffat, 2024). The Sunna on the other hand is essential in shaping the human understanding behind the divinely revealed law of the Sharia. If the Quran provides the broad legal axioms, the Sunna provides the operational parameters, and the Hadith serves as the transmission medium for those parameters, consequently making the Hadith corpora as the primary empirical data through which the Sunna is accessed and reconstructed (Nyazee, 2019).

	In terms of structure, a Hadith consists of a sanad (chain of transmission, literally “support”, plural: isnād) and a matn (content) (Shaffat, 2024). Below is an example of a Hadith and the aforementioned structure:

وَحَدَّثَنِي عَنْ مَالِكٍ، عَنِ ابْنِ شِهَابٍ، عَنْ عَلِيِّ بْنِ حُسَيْنِ بْنِ عَلِيِّ بْنِ أَبِي طَالِبٍ، أَنَّ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ صلى الله عليه وسلم قَالَ ‏"‏ مِنْ حُسْنِ إِسْلاَمِ الْمَرْءِ تَرْكُهُ مَا لاَ يَعْنِيهِ ‏"‏ ‏.‏ 

And it was related to me (Yahya ibn Yahya al-Laythi) from Malik, from Ibn Shihab, from Ali ibn Husayn ibn Ali ibn Abi Talib, that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said, “Part of the excellence of a man's Islam is that he leaves what does not concern him” (Mālik ibn Anas, 1989, Book 47, Hadith 3).

Based on the example Hadith, the part “And it was related to me (Yahya ibn Yahya al-Laythi) from Malik, from Ibn Shihab, from Ali ibn Husayn ibn Ali ibn Abi Talib, that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said” can be considered as the sanad (chain of transmission), while the quote itself, “Part of the excellence of a man’s Islam is that he leaves what does not concern him” is the matn (content). In order to grade Hadiths, the individuals specified in the sanad must have their reliability be verified, and this is done through the meticulous science of ʿIlm ar-Rijal, literally the “Studies of Men” or the more common Sunni and synonymous term, Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl, literally “critiscm and praise”. Essentially, ʿIlm ar-Rijal or Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl functions as a metadata layer for the sanad, providing qualitative attributes such as reliability, memory, and lifespan for each node in the transmission graph (Indra Yuliasti et al., 2025; Ismail, 2024). 2.2 Impact of Hadith Grading

The grading of Hadith carries profound consequences for Islamic law (fiqh), theology (ʿaqida), and especially in everyday Muslim practice. Hadith that are classified as Ṣaḥīḥ (sound) or Ḥasan (good) are considered legally normative and form the second most authoritative source of Islamic jurisprudence after the Quran (Kamali, 2014). Conversely, acting upon Ḍaʿīf (weak) or Mawḍūʿ (fabricated) traditions without qualification risks introducing groundless practices into religious life (Umanah, 2024). The stakes of misclassification are therefore real and can be detrimental, as they directly affect rulings on worship, commerce, family law, and ethics observed by more than 1.9 billion Muslims worldwide.

The reliability of this grading system relies entirely on the truthfulness of the individuals who transmitted these reports across generations. A single unreliable narrator anywhere in the chain is sufficient to downgrade a Hadith, regardless of how credible the surrounding transmitters may be (Kamali, 2014). This sensitivity to individual narrator credibility implies that even small errors in evaluation, whether due to misidentification of a narrator or misapplication of a critical term, can disseminate into downstream jurisprudential decisions. Therefore, the cumulative impact of such errors, particularly in the rapidly expanding digital Hadith landscape, represents a serious epistemological risk that this research directly seeks to mitigate (Umanah, 2024; Sri Wahyuningsih, 2025).

Looking beyond just the religious domain, the science of Hadith grading has also drawn increasing attention from historians, linguists, and information scientists as one of the oldest and most systematic peer-review and source-criticism methodologies in human intellectual history (Usman, 2025). The methodological rigour of classical muhaddithun (Hadith scholars), who developed chains of trust, biographical verification, and cross-referencing centuries before modern academic citation standards, offers a compelling analogue to contemporary knowledge verification challenges (Ghriss, 2025). Applying computational tools to this tradition, therefore, carries interdisciplinary significance that extends well beyond Islamic studies alone (A'thoina & Hasballah, 2025).

2.3 Hadith Authentication Process

The classical process of authenticating a Hadith is a dual-track methodology that evaluates both the sanad (chain of narrators) and the matn (textual body) of a tradition (Kamali, 2014). Sanad criticism, the focus of this research, concerns the continuity and credibility of the transmission chain from its originator (typically the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ) to the collector who recorded it. Each narrator in the chain must be proven to have met the preceding narrator, to have possessed an accurate memory, and to have been morally upright throughout their life. Any break in the chain (inqita') or identified moral failing in a transmitter is sufficient grounds to weaken or reject the report entirely (Ahmed & Anas, 2025).

The grading system that emerges from this process is hierarchical and multi-tiered. At the apex sits the Ṣaḥīḥ (“sound”) Hadith, which satisfies all criteria of chain continuity, narrator credibility, and absence of hidden defects (ʿillah) or contradictions (shudhadh). Below this are degrees of Ḥasan (“good”), where minor shortcomings in narrator memory are present but do not fatally undermine the report. Ḍaʿīf (“weak”) Hadiths fail one or more of these conditions, whereas Mawḍūʿ (“fabricated”) traditions are those for whose narrators deliberate falsehood has been established (Kamali, 2014). This taxonomy forms the classification target for the automated scoring system proposed in this research, where the aim is to computationally approximate the output of this traditional grading process (Ahmad et al., 2025).

The process of collecting the qualitative assessments that inform these grades is documented in a vast body of biographical literature known as kutub ar-rijal (books on narrators). Scholars such as Yahya ibn Maʿin, Ahmad ibn Hanbal, and later Ibn Hajar al-ʿAsqalani dedicated their careers to interviewing, cross-referencing, and documenting the reliability status of thousands of narrators (Mghari et al., 2024; A'thoina & Hasballah, 2025). These books contain the raw Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl labels, i.e. terms of praise and criticism that this project aims to computationally extract, quantify, and aggregate into an automated reliability index.

2.4 ʿIlm ar-Rijal: An Expansive Biographical Study

ʿIlm ar-Rijal (“the science of narrators”) or also synonymously known as Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl (“criticsm and praise”) is one of the most elaborate biographical knowledge systems ever developed in any civilisation (Ahmed & Anas, 2025). At its peak, classical scholars tracked the life histories, teachers, students, travel records, and credibility assessments of over 500,000 narrators, a feat of multi-generational data collection that has no direct parallel in pre-modern scholarship (Usman, 2025). The core output of this science, the biographical dictionary (muʿjam or tabaqat), catalogues narrators by generation, geographic region, or alphabetical order, and assigns each an evaluative standing based on the consensus of prior critics (Maraoui et al., 2022).

The terminology employed within ʿIlm ar-Rijal is highly technical and hierarchically structured. Terms of commendation (taʿdil) range from the superlative Thiqa Thiqa (supremely reliable) down to Saduq (truthful but occasionally erring), with each grade carrying different implications for the admissibility of a narratorʿs reports. Terms of criticism (jarh) range from mild reservations such as Layyin (“soft”, i.e., slightly weak) to severe condemnations such as Kadhdhab (“liar”) or even Dajjal (“arch-deceiver”) (Kamali, 2014). The precise semantic weight of each term and the conditions under which it strengthens or overrides competing assessments are the subject of a dedicated sub-discipline (Mghari et al., 2024), one that this project attempts to operationalise through Semantic Sentiment Quantisation.

A central computational challenge in this science is the problem of resolving narrator identity. The Arabic naming convention comprises a given name (ism), teknonym (kunya), patronym, matronym or a series of it (nasab), tribal affiliation (nisba), and nickname (laqab), which means that a single narrator may be referred to by dozens of orthographic variants across different texts and centuries (Mahmoud et al., 2022). The scholar known canonically as Abu Dawud as-Sijistani, for example, may appear across manuscripts in numerous abbreviated or dialectally inflected forms. Disambiguating these variants into a single canonical identity is a prerequisite for any reliable automated grading system, and is the primary challenge that the Knowledge Graph and NER components of this research are designed to solve (Najeeb, 2022; Mosa, 2025).

2.5 Advancements in Arabic Named Entity Recognition (NER)

Modern processing of classical Arabic text has moved decisively away from rule-based constraints toward deep bidirectional representations. Research by Mahmoud et al. (2022) using the AR-Sanad 280K dataset established a new baseline for narrator disambiguation, proving that large-scale datasets are essential for handling the morphological density of biographical entries.

The current "State of the Art" (SOTA) by Mosa (2025) and Oumer (2025) utilises Pretrained Language Models (PLMs). Alghamdi et al. (2025) demonstrated that Transformer-based models like AraBERT and MARBERT significantly outperform previous iterations in identifying fraudulent content and extracting technical entities. These advancements facilitate high-accuracy Entity Linking, allowing the system to resolve identities across centuries even when names are identical (Mahmoud et al., 2022; Najeeb, 2022).

2.6 Knowledge Graphs (KG) for Entity Disambiguation

A Knowledge Graph (KG) is a structured representation of real-world entities and the semantic relationships between them, encoded as a directed graph of nodes and edges (Ji et al., 2022). In the context of ʿIlm ar-Rijal, this structure is particularly well-suited to the problem of narrator identity resolution: each node represents a canonical narrator identity, edges encode transmission relationships (teacher-student pairs), and node attributes store the accumulated biographical metadata, including Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl assessments drawn from classical kutub ar-rijal (Maraoui et al., 2022; Shafie, 2021). Unlike flat relational databases, KGs allow queries that traverse multi-hop relationships, enabling the system to infer that two orthographically different name mentions in separate manuscripts refer to the same historical individual based on their shared network neighbourhood (Hogan et al., 2021).

Within the domain of digital Hadith studies, several prominent Knowledge Graphs (KGs) have been developed to model the complex interdependencies of isnād chains (Alam, 2021). Systems such as NarratorsKG encode the topological structure of narrator transmission networks, mapping tens of thousands of distinct identities alongside their diverse kunya and nisba variants (Mahmoud et al., 2024). In a typical hybrid retrieval pipeline, an NER model extracts name mentions, which are then resolved against the KG's candidate pool using both local semantic context and global graph signals (Mahmoud et al., 2024). Specifically, graph-level metrics like PageRank centrality are utilised to weigh a narrator's historical prominence; this helps resolve ambiguities where purely lexical matching fails, a common issue in the classical corpus where a single name may refer to five or more distinct individuals (Mosa, 2025). 

More recently, the integration of KGs with Transformer-based models has defined the current state of the art. Mosa (2025) proposed a KG-Transformer framework that encodes narrator node embeddings directly into the attention mechanism of a pre-trained Arabic language model, allowing the model to simultaneously process the local textual context of a name mention and its global relational position within the transmission network. This synergy between structural and semantic representations addresses what earlier approaches treated as separate problems, which are text understanding and graph reasoning, and produces entity disambiguation accuracy that approaches or exceeds 97% on the AR-Sanad 280K-v2 benchmark (Mahmoud et al., 2024; Mosa, 2025). The construction and querying of such a KG therefore constitute a foundational prerequisite for the GNN-based reliability analysis described in the following section.

2.7 Advancements in Graph Neural Networks (GNN) for Isnād Analysis

Traditionally, the "structural integrity" of an isnād was analysed using Social Network Analysis (SNA), focusing on explicit metrics like degree centrality and betweenness to visualise transmission flows (Shafie, 2026; Siddiqui et al., 2023). While useful for visualisation, SNA is limited in its ability to learn latent relational features (Apgar et al., 2023).

The field has recently evolved toward Graph Neural Networks (GNN) and Knowledge Graphs (KG). Mahmoud et al. (2024) introduced narrator identification by utilising graph query embeddings and a knowledge graph (NarratorsKG), while Mosa (2025) proposed a Knowledge Graph-Transformer framework to synergise structure and semantics. Unlike SNA, GNNs can represent a narrator’s "reliability" as a latent feature influenced by their entire neighbourhood of teachers and students. This allows for the detection of "hidden" patterns, such as temporal inconsistencies where a student claims to have heard from a teacher they could not have met, thus automating a core task of the muhaddithun.

A recent master’s thesis (Oumer, 2025) successfully fine‑tuned AraT5 and CAMeLBERT‑CA to turn Hadith text into relational tuples and graph representations. While it is a useful structural work, it explicitly leaves out narrator reliability scoring, entity linking, and any quantification of Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl terms. This project aims to take graphs from the work as a starting point and add the missing “trust layer”, something clearly stated as out of scope in the aforementioned thesis.

2.8 Advancements in Semantic Sentiment Quantisation

A major challenge in automating Hadith grading is translating qualitative scholarly praise (Ta'dil) or criticism (Jarh) into numerical formats. Recent feasibility studies by Ahmad et al. (2024) confirm that Machine Learning is increasingly capable of automating isnād analysis when technical labels are correctly weighted.

Current advancements focus on Semantic Sentiment Quantisation, where technical terms such as Thiqa (reliable), Saduq (truthful), and Munkar (rejected) are mapped to specific reliability weights. Mghari et al. (2024) introduced Narrator2Vec, which uses word embeddings to create dense representations of a narrator’s standing. This allows for a continuous reliability index , where the framework calculates a composite probability score for an entire chain by aggregating the individual quantised weights of each narrator. This move toward "Explainable AI" ensures that the quantitative output remains rooted in traditional Islamic epistemology (I’thoina & Hasballah, 2024). The synthesis of these three advancements, i.e. transformer-based identity resolution, GNN-driven structural analysis, and sentiment-based weight quantisation, forms the multi-modal foundation of this research.

2.9 The Research Gap: Automated Trust & Reliability Scoring

Despite the significant advancements documented in the preceding sections, a critical gap persists at the intersection of these research streams: no published study has yet delivered a complete, end-to-end pipeline that unifies narrator extraction, identity disambiguation, and probabilistic reliability scoring into a single, evaluatable system. The majority of existing work ceases at either the extraction or disambiguation stage, treating the production of a quantified trustworthiness score as a future direction rather than an achieved outcome (Ahmad et al., 2025). While text-based automated classification methods do exist (Tarmom, 2022), what remains absent is an end-to-end system that unifies knowledge graphs with probabilistic reliability scoring.

The separation between textual NLP work and graph-structural work further compounds this gap. Studies employing AraBERT or similar transformer models for NER do not, as a rule, feed their outputs into Knowledge Graph or GNN frameworks for structural validation (Alghamdi et al., 2025). Conversely, graph-based analyses of isnād networks rarely incorporate the semantic richness of biographical Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl texts as node features (Mosa, 2025; Mahmoud et al., 2024). This disciplinary fragmentation means that existing systems capture either the semantic dimension of narrator reliability or the structural dimension, but not both simultaneously. The multi-modal integration that would make automated grading genuinely robust remains a largely unexplored frontier (Ghriss, 2025).

The standardisation of evaluation metrics also presents a significant gap. While individual studies report precision, recall, or F1-scores for their specific sub-tasks, no shared benchmark protocol exists for assessing the accuracy of an end-to-end automated grading system against the ground-truth classifications found in the Kutub as-Sitta (Farooqi et al., 2024). This absence of a common evaluation framework makes it difficult to compare approaches across studies. Establishing such a benchmark protocol is therefore a secondary but also an important contribution of the present research (Mahmoud et al., 2022).

The closest existing work by Oumer (2025) builds a pipeline from Hadith text to a graph of narrators. While it is a real contribution, it does not answer the question that ‘Ilm ar‑Rijal actually asks: given this chain of narrators, how reliable are they? The output in the aforementioned work is a graph, while the aimed output of this project is a graded Hadith (Ṣaḥīḥ/Ḥasan/Ḍaʿīf) with an explainable score. No published study has yet combined semantic sentiment from Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl texts with GNN‑based structural analysis to produce a narrator reliability score.

This project directly targets these gaps by proposing a unified pipeline that integrates transformer-based NER, Knowledge Graph entity linking, GNN structural analysis, and Semantic Sentiment Quantisation into a single framework evaluated against pre-graded Hadith from the canonical collections. This in turn advances the field from the current state of fragmented sub-system studies toward a reproducible and multi-modal solution grounded in both classical Islamic epistemology and contemporary machine learning practice.

CHAPTER 3

PROJECT METHODOLOGY

3.1 Research Design

This project adopts an experimental research design underpinned by the Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM). The central artefact is an end-to-end automated pipeline that ingests classical Arabic biographical text, extracts and disambiguates narrator entities, and produces a continuous reliability score for each narrator, thereby operationalising the traditional science of Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl through computational means. Development proceeds through three sequential stages, each corresponding to a distinct research objective defined in Section 1.4. Each stage produces a testable intermediate output that feeds into the next, ensuring that failures can be isolated and addressed incrementally. 

The model will operate according to these three stages, 1) establishing the data foundation; 2) constructing the extraction and disambiguation engine; and 3) implementing the scoring model and end-to-end evaluation. This framework will add a probabilistic trust score for each narrator and use graph neural networks to evaluate chain‑level reliability, defining the novelty of this project. Together, these phases form the technical pipeline as illustrated in Section 3.5 below.

3.2 Stage 1: Data Ingestion and Preprocessing

3.2.1 Data Sources

The framework ingests data from two primary streams to satisfy both the semantic evaluation of narrators and the structural evaluation of transmission networks:

Biographical Corpora: Digitized classical Arabic biographical evaluation texts (kutub ar-rijāl) are sourced directly from the OpenITI corpus and the Shamela Digital Library. The primary targeted texts are the Taqrīb al-Tahdhīb by Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī and the Tahdhīb al-Kamāl by al-Mizzī. These texts provide the critical Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl labels required to generate the foundational narrator reliability ground-truth index.

Transmission Chain Corpora: To train and evaluate the Named Entity Recognition (NER) boundary parsing modules, the system ingests the Sanadset 650K dataset, which provides 650,986 Hadiths with their respective isnād and book references. Additionally, an evaluation benchmark consisting of 1,000 validated isnād is systematically drawn from the hadith_datasets (obtained from HuggingFace) which contains the important ‘Grade’ column (e.g., "Sahih (Darussalam)").

Used exclusively for ground truth evaluation to serve as an end-to-end testing matrix.

3.2.2 Biographical Text Parsing and Block Extraction

The raw text documents acquired from the OpenITI corpus contain extensive architectural metadata, including custom YAML headers, page number selectors, and editorial comments that present noise to machine learning classifiers.

To isolate individual biographical entries (tarājim), a specialised Regular Expression (Regex) parser is deployed. The algorithm scans for historical entry identifiers and structural patterns typical of OpenITI formatting (e.g., `# $Tarjama` tags or numeric indices followed by narrator names). Once identified, the algorithm isolates each biography as an independent text block, discarding peripheral text, page numbers, and editorial notes.

3.2.3 Two-Tiered Algorithmic Alignment Framework

Because classical Hadith collections lack a universal indexing convention, records cannot be integrated using flat database joins on numerical indices. To cross-reference records accurately and efficiently, this project implements a reproducible, two-tiered hybrid alignment pipeline that combines sparse vector space modeling with token-level edit distance calculations. This architecture drastically reduces the search space, preventing computational bottlenecks while maintaining strict deterministic reproducibility.

Tier 1: Heuristic Search Space Reduction (Information Retrieval Layer)

To avoid computing the Levenshtein distance across a computationally prohibitive  string pairs, a sparse vector retrieval layer is introduced to reduce the search space from the entire population () to a narrow candidate pool ().

The normalised Matn strings of the entire Sanadset 650K corpus are fitted into a sparse TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency) vectorizer using character n-grams () to capture sub-word variations and typos.

A stratified sample of 1,200 Hadiths is drawn from the hadith_datasets HuggingFace corpus, consisting of 200 Hadiths per canonical book from the Kutub as-Sitta (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Sunan Abī Dāwūd, Jāmiʿ at-Tirmiḏī, Sunan al-Nasāʾī, and Sunan Ibn Mājah). This per-book stratification ensures balanced representation across all six collections, providing sufficient statistical power (margin of error ≈ 2.8%) while remaining feasible within the project timeline. Each of the 1,200 sampled Hadiths from the HuggingFace corpus is tokenized and transformed into the same vector space.

A cosine similarity dot product is calculated between the sample vector and the corpus matrix, extracting the top  highest-scoring candidate documents for each sample Hadith within milliseconds.

Reproducibility and Quality Control Constraints

To guarantee that this pipeline can be fully replicated by future researchers, three strict compliance mechanisms are built into the source code:

Deterministic Seeding: The stratified sampling module fixes a global pseudo-random number of generator seed (), ensuring that the identical subset of 1,200 Hadiths is generated on every execution.

Immutable Hashing: Upon a successful match, the records are merged and assigned an unalterable primary key (id) generated via a SHA-256 hash of the normalized consonantal text skeleton.

Quarantine Routing Vector: Any record where the top candidate falls below the 95% fuzzy threshold or yields irreconcilable node mismatches during secondary validation is automatically routed to an isolated JSON quarantine log for manual scholar-in-the-loop remediation, preserving the integrity of the primary training dataset.

3.4 Stage 3: Probabilistic Trust Scoring and Evaluation

3.4.1 Semantic Sentiment Analysis for Continuous Reliability Scoring

The core innovation of this stage is the translation of qualitative Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl labels into a continuous reliability score on the interval [0.0, 1.0], initially developed by Ghriss (2025) in their Iterative Co-Update Algorithm (ICUA) framework. This framework specifically formalises narrator reliability scores () on a scale of 0 to 1. This is achieved through semantic sentiment analysis, an NLP-based approach where it learns from the full contextual wording of scholarly assessments rather than relying on keyword-to-weight lookup alone. This proceeds in two parallel tracks that are compared empirically.

3.4.1.1 Track A: Transformer-Based Semantic Sentiment Classifier

A fine-tuned Arabic transformer model (AraBERT v2) is trained as a regression model to directly predict a reliability score from the raw text of a Jarḥ wa Taʿdīl statement. The training data consists of evaluative sentences extracted from the biographical entries in Taqrīb al-Tahdhīb and Tahdhīb al-Kamāl, where each statement is paired with its corresponding ordinal grade in the classical hierarchy (e.g., Thiqa Thiqa, Thiqa, Ṣadūq, Layyin, Kaḏḏāb). The model architecture is as follows:

Base model: AraBERT v2, a bidirectional transformer pre-trained on ~70B tokens of Modern Standard and Classical Arabic text.

Classification head: A pooling layer (mean of all token embeddings) followed by a linear layer with a single output neuron and a sigmoid activation, producing a score in [0.0, 1.0].

Loss function: Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the predicted score and the normalised ground-truth grade.

Unlike a static lookup table, the transformer model captures semantic context: it learns that "kāna thiqa ḍabt ḥāfiẓ" ("he was reliable, firm, and had a strong memory") carries the same weight as the single term thiqa despite different wording. It can also detect when a positive term is negated (e.g., "laysa bi-thiqa", "he is not reliable"), which a keyword lookup would misclassify.

The biographical entries are then segmented into individual evaluative sentences using Arabic sentence boundary detection (via CAMeL Tools). Each sentence is labelled with the normalised score of its highest-grade term. The model is fine-tuned for 10 epochs with a learning rate of 2 × 10⁻⁵, using the AdamW optimiser with a linear warmup over the first 500 steps and a batch size of 16. A stratified 80/20 train-test split ensures all grade categories are proportionally represented.

Weighted arithmetic mean, where the weight of each assessment is influenced by the historical prominence of the assessing scholar; and

3.4.2 Graph Neural Network for Structural Integrity Analysis
