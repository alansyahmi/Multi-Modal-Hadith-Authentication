# Chapter 2: Literature Review

## 2.1 Impact of Hadith Grading

The grading of Hadith is not merely an academic exercise; it carries profound consequences for Islamic law (fiqh), theology ('aqidah), and everyday Muslim practice (Kamali, 2022). Hadith that are classified as Sahih (sound) or Hasan (good) are considered legally normative and form the second most authoritative source of Islamic jurisprudence after the Quran (Zuhdi, 2025). Conversely, Da'if (weak) or Mawdu' (fabricated) traditions, if acted upon without qualification, risk introducing unsubstantiated practices into religious life (Azizah, 2023). The stakes of misclassification are therefore not abstract — they directly affect rulings on worship, commerce, family law, and ethics observed by over 1.9 billion Muslims globally (Ibrahim, 2025).

The reliability of this grading system hinges entirely on the veracity of the individuals who transmitted these reports across generations (Kamali, 2022). A single unreliable narrator anywhere in the chain is sufficient to downgrade a Hadith, regardless of how credible the surrounding transmitters may be. This sensitivity to individual narrator credibility means that even small errors in evaluation — whether due to misidentification of a narrator or misapplication of a critical term — can propagate into downstream jurisprudential decisions. The cumulative impact of such errors, particularly in the rapidly expanding digital Hadith landscape, represents a serious epistemological risk (Azizah, 2023; Nur, 2022).

Beyond the religious domain, the science of Hadith grading has drawn increasing attention from historians, linguists, and information scientists as one of the oldest and most systematic peer-review and source-criticism methodologies in human intellectual history (Usman, 2025; Azwar & Usman, 2025). The methodological rigour of classical *muhaddithun* (Hadith scholars), who developed chains of trust, biographical verification, and cross-referencing centuries before modern academic citation standards, offers a compelling analogue to contemporary knowledge verification challenges (Brown, 2021). Applying computational tools to this tradition therefore carries interdisciplinary significance that extends well beyond Islamic studies alone (A'thoina & Hasballah, 2025).

## 2.2 Hadith Authentication Process

The classical process of authenticating a Hadith is a dual-track methodology that evaluates both the **Sanad** (chain of narrators) and the **Matn** (textual body) of a tradition (Kamali, 2022; Brown, 2021). Sanad criticism — the focus of this research — concerns itself with the continuity and credibility of the transmission chain from its originator down to the collector who recorded it. Each narrator in the chain must be proven to have met the preceding narrator, to have possessed an accurate memory, and to have been morally upright throughout their life (Setiawan, 2024). Any break in the chain (*inqita'*) or identified moral failing in a transmitter is sufficient grounds to weaken or reject the report entirely (Mahmud, 2026).

The grading system that emerges from this process is hierarchical and multi-tiered. At the apex sits the *Sahih* (sound) Hadith, which satisfies all criteria of chain continuity, narrator credibility, and absence of hidden defects (*'illah*) or contradictions (*shudhadh*). Below this are degrees of *Hasan* (good), where minor shortcomings in narrator memory are present but do not fatally undermine the report (Kamali, 2022). Weak (*Da'if*) Hadiths fail one or more of these conditions, while *Mawdu'* (fabricated) traditions are those for whose narrators deliberate falsehood has been established (Marhamah et al., 2025). This taxonomy forms the classification target for the automated scoring system proposed in this research, where the aim is to computationally approximate the output of this traditional grading process (Tarmom et al., 2022).

The process of collecting the qualitative assessments that inform these grades is documented in a vast body of biographical literature known as *kutub ar-rijal* (books on narrators). Scholars dedicated their careers to interviewing, cross-referencing, and documenting the reliability status of thousands of narrators (Syahabuddin, 2023). These books contain the raw *Jarh wa Ta'dil* labels — terms of praise and criticism — that this project aims to computationally extract, quantify, and aggregate into an automated reliability index (Mghari et al., 2024; A'thoina & Hasballah, 2025).

## 2.3 'Ilm ar-Rijal: An Expansive Biographical Science

*'Ilm ar-Rijal* (the science of narrators) is one of the most elaborate biographical knowledge systems ever developed in any civilisation (Anas, 2025). At its peak, classical scholars tracked the life histories, teachers, students, travel records, and credibility assessments of over 500,000 narrators — a feat of distributed, multi-generational data collection that has no direct parallel in pre-modern scholarship (Usman, 2025; Arwansyah & Rahim, 2025). The core output of this science, the biographical dictionary (*mu'jam* or *tabaqat*), catalogues narrators by generation, geographic region, or alphabetical order, and assigns each an evaluative standing based on the consensus of prior critics (Dallil et al., 2022).

The terminology employed within *'Ilm ar-Rijal* is highly technical and hierarchically structured. Terms of commendation (*ta'dil*) range from the superlative *Thiqah Thiqah* (supremely reliable) down to *Saduq* (truthful but occasionally erring) (Nuraini, 2025). Terms of criticism (*jarh*) span from mild reservations such as *Layyin* (soft, i.e., slightly weak) to severe condemnations such as *Kadhdhab* (liar) (Kamali, 2022). The precise semantic weight of each term, and the conditions under which it strengthens or overrides competing assessments, is the subject of a dedicated sub-discipline — one that this project attempts to operationalise through Semantic Sentiment Quantisation (Mghari et al., 2024).

A central computational challenge posed by this science is the problem of **narrator identity resolution**. The Arabic naming convention — comprising a given name (*ism*), patronymic (*kunya*), lineage chain (*nasab*), tribal affiliation (*nisba*), and epithet (*laqab*) — means that a single narrator may be referred to by dozens of orthographic variants across different texts and centuries (Mahmoud et al., 2022; Najeeb, 2022). Disambiguating these variants into a single canonical identity is a prerequisite for any reliable automated grading system, and is the primary challenge that the Knowledge Graph and NER components of this research are designed to solve (Mosa, 2025).

## 2.4 Advancements in Arabic Named Entity Recognition (NER)
Modern processing of classical Arabic text has moved decisively away from rule-based constraints toward deep bidirectional representations. Research by Mahmoud et al. (2022) using the AR-Sanad 280K dataset established a new baseline for narrator disambiguation, proving that large-scale datasets are essential for handling the morphological density of biographical entries.
The current "State of the Art" (SOTA) utilises Pretrained Language Models (PLMs). Alghamdi et al. (2025) demonstrated that Transformer-based models like AraBERT and MARBERT significantly outperform previous iterations in identifying fraudulent content and extracting technical entities. These advancements facilitate high-accuracy Entity Linking, allowing the system to resolve identities across centuries even when names are identical (Mahmoud et al., 2022; Najeeb, 2022).


## 2.5 Knowledge Graphs (KG) for Entity Disambiguation

A Knowledge Graph (KG) is a structured representation of real-world entities and the semantic relationships between them, encoded as a directed graph of nodes and edges (Ji et al., 2022). In the context of *'Ilm ar-Rijal*, this structure is particularly well-suited to the problem of narrator identity resolution: each node represents a canonical narrator identity, edges encode transmission relationships (teacher-student pairs), and node attributes store the accumulated biographical metadata — including *Jarh wa Ta'dil* assessments — drawn from classical *kutub ar-rijal* (Dallil et al., 2022; Shafie, 2021). Unlike flat relational databases, KGs allow queries that traverse multi-hop relationships, enabling the system to infer that two orthographically different name mentions in separate manuscripts refer to the same historical individual based on their shared network neighbourhood.

The **NarratorsKG** is the most prominent domain-specific KG developed for Hadith isnād analysis. It encodes the topological structure of the narrator transmission network, representing over 18,000 distinct narrator identities along with their known aliases, kunya, and nisba variants (Mahmoud et al., 2024). When an NER model extracts a name mention from an isnād, NarratorsKG is queried to generate a ranked list of candidate identities. Graph-level signals — such as PageRank centrality, which reflects a narrator's historical prominence within the network — are then combined with the NER model's local semantic confidence score to select the most probable canonical match (Mahmoud et al., 2024). This hybrid retrieval process has been shown to substantially outperform purely lexical disambiguation approaches, particularly for narrators whose names are shared by five or more distinct individuals across the classical corpus (Mahmoud et al., 2022).

More recently, the integration of KGs with Transformer-based models has defined the current state of the art. Mosa (2025) proposed a KG-Transformer framework that encodes narrator node embeddings directly into the attention mechanism of a pre-trained Arabic language model, allowing the model to simultaneously process the local textual context of a name mention and its global relational position within the transmission network. This synergy between structural and semantic representations addresses what earlier approaches treated as separate problems — text understanding and graph reasoning — and produces entity disambiguation accuracy that approaches or exceeds 97% on the AR-Sanad 280K-v2 benchmark (Mahmoud et al., 2024; Mosa, 2025). The construction and querying of such a KG therefore constitutes a foundational prerequisite for the GNN-based reliability analysis described in the following section.



## 2.6 Advancements in Graph Neural Networks (GNN) for Isnad Analysis
Traditionally, the "structural integrity" of an isnad was analysed using Social Network Analysis (SNA), focusing on explicit metrics like degree centrality and betweenness to visualise transmission flows (Shafie, 2021; Alam & Schneider, 2021). While useful for visualisation, SNA is limited in its ability to learn latent relational features.
The field has recently evolved toward Graph Neural Networks (GNN) and Knowledge Graphs (KG). Mahmoud et al. (2024) introduced narrator identification by querying isnad graphs, while Mosa (2025) proposed a Knowledge Graph-Transformer framework to synergise structure and semantics. Unlike SNA, GNNs can represent a narrator's "reliability" as a latent feature influenced by their entire neighbourhood of teachers and students. This allows for the detection of "hidden" patterns, such as temporal inconsistencies where a student claims to have heard from a teacher they could not have met, thus automating a core task of the muhaddithun.


## 2.7 Advancements in Semantic Sentiment Quantisation
A major challenge in automating Hadith grading is the translation of qualitative scholarly praise (Ta'dil) or criticism (Jarh) into numerical formats. Recent feasibility studies by Ahmad et al. (2024) confirm that Machine Learning is increasingly capable of automating isnad analysis when technical labels are correctly weighted.
Current advancements focus on Semantic Sentiment Quantisation, where technical terms such as Thiqah (reliable), Saduq (truthful), and Munkar (rejected) are mapped to specific reliability weights. Mghari et al. (2024) introduced Narrator2Vec, which uses word embeddings to create dense representations of a narrator's standing. This allows for a continuous reliability index W[0, 1], where the framework calculates a composite probability score for an entire chain by aggregating the individual quantised weights of each narrator. This move toward "Explainable AI" ensures that the quantitative output remains rooted in traditional Islamic epistemology (A'thoina & Hasballah, 2025). The synthesis of these three advancements, i.e. transformer-based identity resolution, GNN-driven structural analysis, and sentiment-based weight quantisation, forms the multi-modal foundation of this research.


Despite the significant advancements documented in the preceding sections, a critical gap persists at the intersection of these research streams: no published study has yet delivered a complete, end-to-end pipeline that unifies narrator extraction, identity disambiguation, and probabilistic reliability scoring into a single, evaluatable system. The majority of existing work terminates at either the extraction or disambiguation stage, treating the production of a quantified trustworthiness score as a future direction rather than an achieved outcome (Mahmoud et al., 2025). The feasibility of automated Hadith grading has been acknowledged, but a concrete and reproducible implementation against established canonical benchmarks remains absent from the literature (Tarmom, 2022).

The separation between textual NLP work and graph-structural work further compounds this gap. Studies employing AraBERT or similar transformer models for NER do not, as a rule, feed their outputs into Knowledge Graph or GNN frameworks for structural validation (Alghamdi et al., 2025). Conversely, graph-based analyses of isnād networks rarely incorporate the semantic richness of biographical *Jarh wa Ta'dil* texts as node features (Mosa, 2025; Mahmoud et al., 2024). This disciplinary fragmentation means that existing systems capture either the semantic dimension of narrator reliability or the structural dimension — but not both simultaneously. The multi-modal integration that would make automated grading genuinely robust remains a largely unexplored frontier (Ghriss, 2025).

The standardisation of evaluation metrics also presents a significant gap. While individual studies report precision, recall, or F1-scores for their specific sub-tasks, no shared benchmark protocol exists for assessing the accuracy of an end-to-end automated grading system against the ground-truth classifications found in *Al-Kutub al-Sittah* (Farooqi et al., 2024). This absence of a common evaluation framework makes it difficult to compare approaches across studies or to claim generalised progress toward the broader goal. Establishing such a benchmark protocol is therefore a secondary but important contribution of the present research (Mahmoud et al., 2022).

This project directly targets these gaps by proposing a unified pipeline that integrates transformer-based NER, Knowledge Graph entity linking, GNN structural analysis, and Semantic Sentiment Quantisation into a single framework evaluated against pre-graded Hadith from the canonical collections. In doing so, it advances the field from the current state of fragmented sub-system studies toward a reproducible, multi-modal solution grounded in both classical Islamic epistemology and contemporary machine learning practice (A'thoina & Hasballah, 2025).
